Identity Continuity

How does the human mind make sense of the world? Our conjecture is that we do two things. First, we identify something. Then we apply continuity to it: after something has happened to it, we still recognise it as the same thing.

This project goes deep into that idea. We want to see whether it can be turned into a general engine: one that learns about the world it is in, and can successfully use what it has learned.

Identity

There is a well-known thought experiment called the Ship of Theseus. Theseus has a ship, and over the years its parts wear out and are replaced one at a time, until none of the original is left. We still call it Theseus's ship. Now suppose someone kept all the old parts and built a second ship out of them. Which of the two is Theseus's ship?

We can argue either way, and that is what makes the puzzle useful. In the real world there is no such thing as Theseus's ship, and no such thing as a plank. There is only a collection of matter that we have identified, in our heads, as one unit. That is what we mean by identity: something we have picked out and labelled as a thing.

Which ship is his depends on what we choose to follow: the ship that was sailed and repaired, or the original material. Following a thing through change like this is the second step, continuity.

Continuity

Here is another thought experiment. Let's assume there is a blue curtain. A red ball rolls in on one side, and a blue ball rolls out on the other.

We did not see what happened behind the curtain. Even so, we take it to be the same ball, and we take the curtain to have turned it blue. We do not imagine that the red ball vanished and a different one appeared.

Now assume a second curtain, a striped one. A ball rolls in and a cube rolls out. We read it the same way: it is the same thing, it is a cube now, and the striped curtain did that.

This is continuity. We identify what came out as the same thing that went in, and we form an idea of what happened to it in between. That idea also tells us what to expect the next time something goes through.

Hierarchy of assumptions

We never saw behind the curtain, so what we hold about it is an assumption. We assume it was the same ball, and we assume the curtain will do the same thing again. Each time that turns out right, the assumption gets stronger.

How much does one assumption cover? We saw one red ball come out blue. Suppose we now have a white ball and want to turn it blue, or a cat. Would the curtain work? It depends on how far we take what we saw, and we can take it at several levels, from general to specific:

  • Any object that goes through comes out blue.
  • Any ball that goes through comes out blue.
  • A red ball that goes through comes out blue.

We hold all of these together, and that is the hierarchy of assumptions. The general ones are what let us answer for things we have never tried: "any ball" covers the white ball, and "any object" covers the cat.

Now suppose we try a gold ball, and it comes out gold. We keep "any ball comes out blue", because it is still right for every other ball. Beneath it we add what we just saw, "the gold ball comes out gold", and for the gold ball we follow that one. A more specific assumption like this is only added when an experiment shows it.

When a more specific assumption applies, we follow it. Otherwise we fall back on a more general one. This is also how we handle being wrong: we add something more specific beneath what we already believed, and the rest stays in place. This way every assumption in the hierarchy can be fully true within its own range, and we never have to settle for one that is only true most of the time.

Curiosity

Now assume a third curtain, a dotted one, and nobody has told us what it does. We want to roll the ball through and see.

That is curiosity. We have something we have identified, the ball, and something that could happen to it, but no assumption about the result. Curiosity is that missing assumption.

Once we have rolled the ball through, we have an assumption and we are less curious. After a few more times the assumption is strong, and our attention moves to something we know less about.

The hierarchy matters here too. When we only have a general assumption about something, and nothing specific, some curiosity remains. After the gold ball came out gold, we want to try a silver one.

Reasoning

Now a question. We have a red ball and we want a blue cube. How do we get one?

Most people answer straight away: put it through the blue curtain, and then through the striped one.

Nobody has shown us a blue cube, or anything passing through two curtains. We got there by treating it as one thing the whole way, and applying our assumptions one after another, each from where the last one left it. That is reasoning: continuity applied again and again until it reaches what we want.

The goal

The goal is an engine that works the same way. It is not a language model and it is not trained in advance. We put it in a world it knows nothing about. It explores because it has no assumptions yet, it builds a hierarchy of assumptions from what it sees, and it uses them in sequence to reach the goals we give it. It keeps learning the whole time, and because what it holds are assumptions, we can read them.

We start with small worlds: a line, a grid, and a grid with a wall. By the end of Part 1 the engine learns all three from nothing and reaches every goal in our tests. What it ends up holding about the wall has the same shape as the gold ball: one general continuity, with more specific ones beneath it.

So far the engine is given its one identity, itself. Learning to identify things on its own is still ahead. Board games come after that. We want an engine that can watch a few games of chess without knowing anything about it, work out how the pieces move, and then play. We are aiming for understanding more than strength: a player like a child after a few evenings, who can then do the same with the next game.
