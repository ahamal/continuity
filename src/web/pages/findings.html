Findings

We built four agents from the conjecture and ran each of them in three small worlds. This page puts together what they showed, and what has to come next.

The numbers are runs that reached the goal, out of 500. In each run the agent explores first and is then sent to a goal. For the third and fourth agents these numbers say less than they seem to. Their exploring hardly uses chance, so on the line and on the grid with a wall the 500 runs are one run repeated.

Two more agents were built along the way and have no page of their own. One stores every combination but answers like the first agent, and gets the same results as the first. The other has its hierarchy built by a procedure we wrote by hand, to see what we were aiming for. It also reaches the goal in every run.

What worked

  1. The conjecture can be built. The fourth agent starts with no knowledge of its world and no training. It learns a line, a grid, and a grid with a wall. On the grid with a wall it reaches the goal on all 500 trips to cells picked at random, and its routes are 8% longer than the shortest there are.
  2. General assumptions reach places the agent has never been. After a few moves, "going right adds one to x" is enough to plan a route across cells it has not visited. That was already true of the first agent.
  3. The hierarchy comes out of counting. We did not tell the third agent which facts matter. It wrote every combination, and the rows about a move ended up on top because they need the fewest specific rows beneath them. For the grid with a wall, 18 rows do all the answering, where the first agent needed 388.
  4. General and specific rows work together when the specific one answers. The second agent let the rows vote and did worse than the first. The third agent uses the same rows and follows the most specific one, so a general row and a specific row beneath it no longer compete.
  5. Only a row that has held every time should answer for the general one. The third agent followed "going right at x = 4 changes nothing" after it had held only 3 times out of 5, and kept going round the wall the long way. The fourth agent drops a row the first time it fails, and takes the shorter way. The edge, which has never failed, stays one row. Every row that answers is then right every time within its own range.
  6. Being wrong needs nothing extra. A wrong prediction is written like any other move, and because it is more specific, it answers from then on. The agent does have to try its guesses, though. The third and fourth agents go to the moves they are least sure about, and that is how they find the wall.

What is missing

The agents learned the wall as a set of separate cases, and never as one idea. "Something is in the way" cannot be said with x and y, so the wall is ten unrelated rows. Nothing learned at one side of it helps at the next side, at another wall, or in another game.

The first two are measured. The last two we have not built yet.

Better facts have a cost. The third and fourth agents write every combination of the facts in a move, and a fact about the state appears in a move twice, before and after. So each fact added multiplies the rows by four:

So better facts make the hierarchy smaller, and they make writing everything down impossible. Writing every combination let us look at what is there without choosing in advance. It cannot be how the engine works in the end.

Both problems lead back to the first half of the conjecture. Every agent here was handed its one identity and the facts that describe it. Everything they learned is the second half, continuity. Identifying things, and finding the facts worth keeping about them, is still ahead. These are the questions:

  1. Where do facts such as "what is next to me" come from, if they are not given?
  2. Which facts are worth putting together in a row, when trying every combination is not possible?
  3. How do things other than the agent enter the state, and how are they grouped into kinds?
  4. Which built-in notions are enough for several games: thing, place, same, direction, distance, before and after?
  5. Which parts of a game can be found, and which have to be told, such as castling being forbidden once the king has moved?
